Annales des Ponts et Chaussées · March–April 1849

The bridge that is only half built by its toll

In 1849 the French state engineer Jules Dupuit asked a plain question: what does a toll do to the usefulness of a bridge, a canal or a railway? His answer introduced consumer surplus, deadweight loss, the revenue curve and the economics of price discrimination, worked out with tables of numbers rather than calculus.

J. Dupuit, De l'influence des péages sur l'utilité des voies de communication · dated Angers, 28 March 1849

37of 100 cross
74toll revenue
93kept by users
81utility lost

Each figure is one potential traveller. Teal walkers value the crossing more than the toll and pay; brick-red walkers value it less and turn back. Their lost trips cost the owner nothing to provide, yet nobody gets that value. Dupuit called it utilité perdue. Move the slider to see what happens to all four numbers.

Context

Where this paper sits

Dupuit was replying to a public quarrel inside the corps of state engineers. The ideas then sat largely unused until economists rediscovered them decades later.

    Finding 1

    Utility, price and cost are three different numbers

    J.-B. Say held that the price of a thing measures its utility. Dupuit's critic Bordas followed him, and so did the engineer Comoy, who valued a canal by its cost per tonne carried.

    Dupuit separates three properties of every good. In his example, a bottle of wine has utility 20 F (the most the buyer would pay), value 6 F (the price paid) and production cost 3 F. A metre of cloth can have utility 30, price 15 and cost 20: the cost can be higher than the price.

    "The state would thus be poorer because the canal cost less! Is it not obvious that it would be richer by all that it had spent less?"Dupuit on Comoy's method, p. 6

    Comoy computed (interest + upkeep) ÷ tonnage = 1.90 F a tonne and called it "the value of the unit of wealth". By that formula, a more skilful engineer who built the same canal for less would appear to have created less wealth. The cost of the canal has to be subtracted from its benefit. It cannot measure it.

    Comoy's paradox, live

    500,000 tonnes a year, interest at 5%. Assume users would pay up to 1.5M F a year for the service; that figure is ours, for illustration. Lower the cost and compare the two measures.

    1.90Comoy "value of the unit of wealth" (F/t)
    550,000Dupuit net benefit = utility − cost (F/yr)

    Comoy's number falls when the canal gets cheaper. Dupuit's net benefit rises.

    Finding 2

    Utility is the largest sacrifice someone would make

    Bordas said utility "is not susceptible of measure". Dupuit replies that it is as obvious as a theorem of geometry. The butcher says a kilo of meat costs 1 franc. Ask whether the customer would still buy at 21 sous, 22, 23 and so on. If they buy at 40 and refuse at 41, the kilo's utility to them is 40 sous, to within a sou. (20 sous = 1 franc; 1 sou = 5 centimes.)

    That number is different for each person: Rossi's bookseller finds buyers at 10 F, at 100 F, and at every price between. It is also different for the same person: a working household buys 3 pounds of meat at 10 sous and only 2 at 15, so the third pound is worth less to them than the first two.

    Paying a price leaves a remainder. Dupuit would pay up to 15 sous to cross the bridge for an urgent meeting. If the toll is 1 sou, he keeps 14 sous of utilité relative. Today this is called consumer surplus.

    Relative utility (consumer surplus) of one use urel=uabs−p

    Finding 3

    Adding up utility from the demand schedule

    For goods used many times, such as a bridge or a canal, utility is the sum over every use. Dupuit's procedure (pp. 25, 37) has four steps:

    1. Write the loi de consommation, the quantity used at each price. It has to be learned from experience.
    2. Raise a tax by small steps, from zero until nothing is used.
    3. The units that disappear when the tax goes from k−1 to k were worth about k each. Multiply.
    4. Add the products. The total is the utility of the whole quantity used at price zero.

    His canal: 100 tonnes at toll 0, 70 at toll 1 (30 tonnes worth 1 F each), 50 at toll 2 (20 tonnes worth 2 F each), and so on to zero at toll 10. The utility totals 330.

    He also assumes the schedule is convex. Each price cut gains more users than the last because a cheaper good reaches a wider part of society. So the gaps between successive quantities shrink as price rises.

    Dupuit's sum, and its modern continuous form U=∑k=1nk·(Qk−1−Qk)≈∫0∞Q(s)ds

    Build the canal's utility, step by step

    Each block is a batch of tonnes that a 1-franc rise in the toll drives off the canal. Width = tonnes lost, height = the toll at which they leave, area = their utility.

    Finding 4

    Say's method overstates every gain

    Bordas valued a price cut as (price cut × all units now used). Take his stone example: a canal cuts stone from 20 F to 15 F a tonne and the town's use rises from 10,000 to 15,000 tonnes. Say's method gives 5 × 15,000 = 75,000 F.

    Dupuit points out that the 5,000 new tonnes cannot each be worth 5 F, or they would have been bought at 20 F. They go to uses that only become worthwhile as stone gets cheaper: garden walls at 19 F, sheds for the rich at 18 F, for the less rich at 16 to 17 F, paved streets at 15 F. Raising a hypothetical toll 1 F at a time gives 62,100 F.

    "You say I gained 3,000 francs on my stockings; take them and give me half my gain."Dupuit's worker, answering the claim that cheap stockings made him rich, p. 35

    Queen Elizabeth's single pair of knitted stockings was worth perhaps 1,000 F. A worker owns three pairs at 3 F. At 6 F he would own two, at 10 F one. Valuing all three pairs at the old price confuses the price drop with the gain.

    The same error applies to the Pont d'Arcole: traffic rose tenfold after its toll was abolished. Say's rule would value the abolition at ten times the old toll revenue, yet only one crosser in ten gains the full sou.

    Finding 5 · the centrepiece

    Every toll cuts utility into three parts

    What the numbers show

    Revenue rises, peaks and falls. At toll 0 it is zero; at a high enough toll nobody crosses and it is zero again. In between there is one toll with the highest revenue, and every lower revenue is reached by two tolls. Dupuit calls this "a general property of all taxes on consumption".

    Utility falls more slowly than traffic at first. On Bridge A, moving from toll 0 to 1 loses 45% of crossings but only 18% of utility. The first crossings lost are the ones people valued least.

    Equal utility, different crossings. On Bridge B at toll 3, the 65 trips that no longer happen are worth 115. That is about what the 35 trips actually made bring in revenue. Counting heads does not measure usefulness.

    A slip in the original. Dupuit prints the revenue of Bridge B at toll 3 as 115 and calls it the maximum. Since 3 × 35 = 105, the 115 was copied from the lost-utility column. Consumer surplus at that toll is then 112, not his 102. Toll 3 is still the revenue maximum and none of his conclusions change. The page computes every figure from the demand schedule, so it shows 105.
    Finding 6

    Who owns the bridge decides how useful it is

    It was often said that a concession company's interest was the same as the state's. Dupuit's tables refute this.

    • A company sets the toll with the highest revenue: 2 on A, 3 on B, 5 on C.
    • The state wants only interest, upkeep and amortisation. Bridge C cost 150,000 F: 6,000 F interest at 4% plus 2,000 F upkeep = 8,000 F, and toll 1 already brings in 8,000 F (with the schedule scaled so 100 means 10,000 crossings).

    The company takes 8,500 F more from users. Dupuit says this hardly matters for public wealth: shareholders gain exactly what crossers pay, so the money only changes hands. The 14,900 F of utility is different. People who would have crossed don't, although carrying them would have cost the company nothing. Nobody gets that value.

    Uncertainty strengthens the case for the state. A company that cuts its toll and is wrong about demand takes a real loss. If the state is wrong, the money stays in its citizens' pockets, and the state can collect it through another tax.

    Brick: value lost for good. Ochre: money moved from users to shareholders. Only the brick bar makes the country poorer.

    Scenario: the Pont d'Arcole, Paris

    A report to the Seine municipal council found that crossings on the Pont d'Arcole rose tenfold after its toll was abolished. Dupuit takes the old toll as 5 and the new traffic as 100 against the old 10. That matches Bridge A, and with it he answers the council's question: should the toll be restored?

    "Tariffs build bridges, canals and railways without spending a penny. They can also destroy them. It would be as well to demolish the Pont d'Arcole as to set its toll at 100 francs."p. 49
    Finding 7

    Charging different people different prices

    The best tariff, Dupuit says, would make each user pay a share of their own utility, for example half of the price that would stop them crossing. Nobody would be priced off the bridge, so no utility would be lost, and revenue would still cover the cost.

    Dupuit's ideal toll for user i with maximum price vi τi=αvi,0<α<1

    Users won't honestly state their maximum, "the universal improbity of travellers" as Dupuit puts it. So a real tariff guesses each user's maximum from signs it can observe and sorts users into classes.

    The bookseller publishes at 100 F, then 50, 30, 20, 15, 10 and finally 6 F. Bordas called this fraud. Dupuit replies that it lets the publisher pay the author 100,000 F for a manuscript that once fetched a few hundred, while readers with 6 F still get the book. The first buyers chose freely, and many would have paid 150 F.

    The theatre prices seats by the "caprices" of spectators, those who come to see and those who come to be seen, far more than by sight lines.

    Canal freight. The 1821–22 canals charged per tonne by type of good: manure, sand and gravel 0.010 F up to fabrics 0.088 F. The expensive goods then went by road. Dupuit would charge each good by what transport by canal is worth to its shipper: coffee 2, coal 4 and fertiliser 6. Coffee leaves for the road above 3 F. Fertiliser has no alternative at all, since carting it over land costs far more.

    "Tolls, like indirect taxes, are voluntary taxes. You can make the poor pay less, but you cannot make the rich pay more."p. 56–57

    Scenario: the workers' footbridge

    A footbridge cost 150,000 F and earns only 5,000 F a year at 5 centimes. Workers detour around it every morning and evening because 10 centimes a day is too much on their wages. The new owner adds one clause: "for passers in cap, smock or jacket, the toll is 1 centime." That brings 300,000 new crossings a year at 1 c. Some old payers will dress the part to pay less.

    Dupuit's numbers: 5 c only → 5,000 F; 1 c for all → 4,000 F; the two-tier tariff → 6,400 F, and close to 8,000 F if a stricter rule (only at shift times, only with a worker's livret) stops the leakage.

    Finding 8

    The railway with classes

    Give a railway Bridge C's demand schedule and a traction cost of 2 per traveller. With traction costs, the fare with the highest net receipts is 6 (net 104), higher than the gross maximum at 5. Fixed costs are interest and administration. If they are 110, a single fare loses money whatever it is set at.

    The line is still useful: at fare 6 it produces 234 of utility (52 traction, 104 to the company, 78 left to travellers). Add a second class and the same demand does much better. With fares 4 and 7: 41 travellers, net receipts 142, traveller surplus 92, a profit of 32. "There is gain for everybody."

    Net receipts of a two-class tariff (low l, high h, traction cost c) Π=(h−c)Q(h)+(l−c)[Q(l)−Q(h)]

    The catch: some of those 20 travellers willing to pay 7 will take the 4-franc seat if they can. The wider the gap between fares, the more trade down. Dupuit estimates that (3, 7) falls from 130 to 106 net, while (4, 7) only falls to 130.

    This explains the third-class wagons of his day, open to the weather, badly sprung, with hard benches. A roof would cost little, and the state could even pay for it. The company would refuse because better third-class wagons could cost it two million in receipts.

    "They strike at the poor, not from any wish to make them suffer, but to frighten the rich."p. 65

    Finding 9

    Tolls should not be proportional to distance

    Every French tariff of 1849 charged per kilometre. Dupuit calls this "a very grave error". What a trip is worth depends on the alternatives, not on the length of track. Charging for "distance usefully travelled" would still be wrong.

    Châteauroux to Tours by rail went round through Orléans: 259 km, 19 F, about 7 hours. The road took 9 to 10 hours and cost about 10 F. At 19 F travellers chose the road. At 10 F they would ride in seats that would otherwise run empty, and almost all the fare would be profit. The posts already charged 20 c for a letter to Versailles and 4 c for a newspaper to Marseille.

    The longest trip gets the lowest fare, which is fine. Third-class fares can work the same way: 0.50 F for the 10 km to a station used by rich villa owners, 0.60 F for the 20 km to a farming village with other ways into town.

    Route planning follows. A longer line through busier, flatter country can carry a lower fare per kilometre. Dupuit's example: 200 km at 0.06 F costs a traveller less than 175 km at 0.08 F.

    Another slip. Dupuit writes 175 km × 0.08 = 16 F; it is 14 F. The saving on the longer line is 2 F per traveller rather than 4 F, and the point stands.

    Town V and the line S–T (his Fig. 8)

    Every mode costs 0.08 F/km. A traveller from V to X can take the road to T (50 km) or the road to S (30 km) then the train S→T (40 km). At the per-km fare of 3.20 F, the train loses them. Lower the S→T fare for travellers coming from V.

    Equations

    Dupuit wrote no formulas; he used tables and arithmetic. These are the standard modern forms of his results, with Q(t) the number of uses at toll t.

    On a staircase demand, the integrals become Dupuit's sums: the units lost between tolls k−1 and k each count as worth k. That is the convention the tables, charts and code on this page use.
