Part I pp. 56–57

The wall in the dark

Engineers of the 1840s valued France's royal roads at 500 million francs a year, which was what society paid for the transport on them. They reasoned, with J.-B. Say, that if people pay 500 million they must get 500 million of satisfaction.

Dupuit asks what happens when a better road halves the cost. Say's rule now says the roads are worth 250 million, so they became less useful by improving. In fact their utility rose by at least 250 million.

A man measuring a wall in the dark finds he cannot reach the top, and declares: this wall is two metres high.Dupuit, p. 57 (paraphrased)

The hand stopping at 2 m proves only that the wall is at least 2 m. A price is the same kind of fact: a lower bound on utility, never its measure.

Price = how high your hand reaches

"Utility ≥ 500 million" is true. "Utility = 500 million" is the mistake.

Part II pp. 59–61

Every hectolitre has a different value

A hilltop town hauls its water up by hand: one hectolitre a day costs a 50-franc yearly subscription. Public pumps lower the price step by step. The same household buys more each time, but each extra hectolitre goes to a less pressing need: drinking first, then washing, the garden, a pond, and finally a fountain that runs all day.

So it is wrong to say the pumped water is "worth 50 F a hectolitre". Only the first hectolitre is. Pick a price and watch which needs are met.

Dupuit's test: raise the price again with a tax. A 4 F tax on 1 F water cuts use from 100 to 20 hl, a 9 F tax from 20 to 10, and so on back up to one hectolitre at 50 F. The tax that makes a unit disappear tells you what that unit was worth. You have just drawn a demand curve.

Part III pp. 65–67

Absolute and relative utility

Absolute utility is the largest sacrifice a buyer would make to get the thing. Relative utility is what is left after paying: the gain the buyer pockets. Today we call it consumer surplus.

Dupuit's general law (p. 67): a price change moves the utility of buyers who stay by exactly the change. Buyers who leave lose their whole surplus, and buyers who arrive gain theirs. A tax hurts people who never pay it, because they stop buying.

Part IV pp. 68–76, 82–83

Navier's canal and the 27-fold error

The engineer Navier valued canals by transport savings: a tonne costs 1 F per league by road and 0.13 F by canal, so the canal "creates" 0.87 F per tonne per league. Dupuit takes the method apart with a city that uses 10,000 tonnes of building stone a year.

Where the stone comes fromdelivered cost per tonne

Haulage goes up from 4 F to 13 F, yet the stone gets cheaper. A good transport link changes where goods come from.

Utility of the canal for stone, two methods
Navier: 30,000 t × 100 leagues × 0.87 F2,610,000 F
Dupuit: 50,000 F (old tonnes) + 46,000 F (new tonnes)96,000 F

The four errors

  1. Comparing transport costs instead of production costs. The real gain per old tonne is 20 − 15 = 5 F. Navier gives 87 F, which is 17 times too much.
  2. Giving new tonnes the full saving. Cheaper stone draws in 20,000 more tonnes, but those buyers refused it at 20 F, so each gains between 0 and 5 F. A rising imaginary tax shows the average is 2.30 F.
  3. Valuing new products at their old impossible price. Slate that "would have cost 1,000 F by mule" and now costs 20 F is not worth 980 F to anyone, because no one bought it at 1,000.
  4. Ignoring quality. Roads are faster and safer. If a merchant switches to the canal for a few centimes' net gain, those centimes are the true utility.

Dupuit's rule (p. 76): the utility of a product is the tax that would stop its consumption. Raise an imaginary tax in small steps tk, multiply each by the units Δqk it drives away, and add. In the limit (his footnote 12 says the calculus is exact) this is the area under the demand curve P(q).

The footbridge simulator

A free footbridge carries 2,080,000 crossings a year. Dupuit imagines tolls in 1-centime steps and counts how many crossings each one would remove. Each block below is a group of walkers. Total utility = toll revenue + walkers' surplus + deadweight loss, shown with Dupuit's figures for a 0.05 F toll. The toll collector's share is not lost to society; it only moves money around. The red area is gone for good. Dupuit compares it to friction in a machine: walkers who valued the Pont des Arts at 0.04, 0.03 or 0.02 F walk round by the Pont-Neuf instead (p. 90).

Things to try: at 0 F the whole 102,000 F goes to walkers. Revenue peaks at 0.04 F (38,720 F), yet 0.05 F raises almost as much (38,500 F) while losing more. At 0.15 F revenue and utility are both zero. Dupuit's conclusion is that tolls are neither always good nor always bad, and should be set by principle so they cover costs while destroying as little utility as possible.

Part VI pp. 84–89

The square law of taxes

Put a 5 F tax back on the canal's stone. Use falls to 10,000 tonnes and the tax raises 50,000 F, which is only a transfer to the state. The 20,000 tonnes no longer bought take away 46,000 F of utility that nobody receives. For small taxes, the loss grows with the square of the rate.

The revenue curve is a hill, so any amount below the peak can be raised by two rates, one low and one high, with very different losses (p. 89). This is a Laffer curve, 130 years early.

Left: the loss from tax t is proportional to t², so a 10 F tax destroys about 100 times what a 1 F tax does. Right: Dupuit's upper bound (p. 88). Demand grows faster as price falls, like a pyramid of cannonballs that is widest at the bottom, so a price change Δp that adds or removes Δq units moves utility by at most half their product. Check: ½ × 5 F × 20,000 t = 50,000 F, above the true 46,000 F.

Part VII pp. 79–81

Real gains or just transfers?

Dupuit's rule: unless quality changes, utility is created only when production costs fall. A price change on its own moves money from sellers to buyers or back. The state should care who wins and who loses, but these moves don't belong in the utility count. Sort each case.

Where the paper sits

  

    
1776
Adam Smith separates value in use from value in exchange.

    
1803
J.-B. Say's Traité: price is the measure of utility.

    
1832
Navier values canals at 0.87 F saved per tonne-league.

    
1844
Dupuit: relative utility, the tax-sum method, deadweight loss, the t² law.

    
1849
Dupuit's follow-up on tolls and differential pricing.

    
1890
Marshall names it consumer's surplus and draws the triangle.

    
1936
US Flood Control Act: benefits must exceed costs.

    
1964
Harberger popularises the "triangle" for measuring tax waste.

    
1974
The Laffer curve: revenue first rises with the rate, then falls.
