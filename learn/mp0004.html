Private Monopolies & Public Rights

Arthur Twining Hadley (1856–1930)pp. 28–44jstor.org/stable/1883107

"The history of transportation law has constituted an exception to the general laws of the past, but it may not improbably constitute an indication as to the general laws of the future."

1 problemThe railroad is at once highway and carrier.
4 solutionsTried by England, Prussia, the U.S. and the courts.
2 toolsEquality of treatment and publicity of management.
3 phasesCompete → dictate → regulated combination.

The argument in one paragraph. Big firms in industries with heavy fixed investment tend toward monopoly, and laws trying to force them back into competition mostly fail. Railroads met this problem first and hardest. Lawmakers tried keeping road and carrier apart, then state ownership, then building rival lines. Each failed or caused new harm. What worked best was to accept combination as inevitable and attach public rights to it: equal treatment of customers and public disclosure of how the firm is run. Hadley predicted that factories and other capital-heavy industries would take the same path.

How to use this page. Read the sections in order. Each one covers a single step of the argument and has a diagram or simulator you can adjust. Step 1 · pp. 28–29

Highway versus carrier

The old law split transportation into two things with opposite rules. The highway (road or river) is a natural monopoly, so it stays public. The carrier (whoever drives the wagon or sails the boat) is private, and competition keeps its charges fair.

Step 2 · pp. 29–31

Three strains on the old theory

Three developments broke the clean split between public road and private carrier.

i

Service needed organization

Some carrying could only be done well by one organized body. Letter carriage became a monopoly at least two hundred years before Hadley wrote (run by the State in France, privately for a time in Germany and England). Trade with the East Indies needed companies stronger than any single merchant, and Holland and England gave them sweeping privileges.

Carrier → monopoly
ii

Better highways cost money

Turnpikes, canals and wharves were expensive. Paying for them through taxes was an "intolerable burden." France did it and caused deep public grievances. England and America left improvements to private enterprise, so private capital "acquired new rights over the highway."

Highway → private
iii

Rail and telegraph merged both

Each needed organized service and a large permanent investment. Most importantly, the line and the business worked best "owned and controlled by the same head." Highway and carrier became a single firm.

Highway = carrier
Step 3 · pp. 31–39 · the core framework

Four ways out

Finding 1 · pp. 32–33

Why outside carriers lose

Under Option 1, the railroad charges outside carriers a toll for using the track, and the carriers compete with each other. Hadley explains why this fails. A railroad's cost has two parts: fixed charges (interest and maintenance) and transportation expenses (the cost of actually moving goods). An outside carrier must charge at least its running cost plus the toll. The railroad can charge anything above its running cost, because every extra dollar helps pay the fixed charges.

Presets:

A fixed toll "would raise rates so high as to stop a large part of the existing traffic in coal, in lumber, and even in wheat." Having the legislature revise tolls from time to time does not help either. No one would invest in a railroad whose earnings could be reset by law, and no railroad would expand if it "would thus lose all fruit from its gains."

Finding 2 · pp. 34–36

Cut-throat competition

Option 3 was to build parallel lines and let them compete. Hadley argues that when railroads compete, rates fall to roughly operating cost and nothing is left to pay fixed charges. This is not because managers are reckless. It follows from the economics, for two reasons.

1 · Fixed charges are like rent

A farmer about to lose his market will still sell wheat for slightly more than it cost to grow, even if the price pays nothing toward rent. In the same way, a railroad about to lose traffic will cut "almost to the level of operating expenses," leaving nothing for interest.

2 · No diminishing returns

Farming eventually runs into diminishing returns. Railroads do not: the more traffic a road takes, the cheaper each ton becomes. That means "there is thus no direct limit to this cut-throat competition."

Finding 3 · pp. 35–36, 40

The boom-and-bust cycle

A rate war "must end in consolidation, followed by a restoration of prices to a non-competitive figure." High rates then attract new capital, a new competitor is built, and the war begins again. Hadley sees the same pattern in factories, and notes that "with each change, the chance for the small independent business man is less."

Finding 4 · pp. 34–36, 38–39

Shippers lose too

Competing lines cost investors heavily, and shippers get little in return. Where competition hits a few points, it causes "enormous local differences or discriminations." Where it hits all points, rates swing wildly. Hadley's key claim: "equality and steadiness of rates are more important to the business community than mere low average charges."

The speculator ships only in the three cheapest months. The ordinary merchant ships the same amount every month. Rates are illustrative, in dollars per ton.

Three drawbacks of competing highways (p. 34)

1

Often physically impossible. The first route is usually the best, sometimes the only one, and a newcomer struggles to get equal terminal facilities.

2

Waste for the investor. Most roads could carry far more traffic than they have. Splitting it across two lines "is just so much waste."

3

No gain to the shipper. Where competition does happen, the investor's loss is "not compensated by any corresponding gain to the shipper,—rather the reverse."

Finding 5 · pp. 36–38

The Granger cases: a right, not a method

The U.S. Supreme Court's Granger decisions (led by Munn v. Illinois, 1877, about Chicago grain elevators) broke with legal tradition, "justified by the logic of facts rather than by legal precedents." In theory, railroads and elevators were governed by free competition. In fact, they were monopolies, and that created a right to regulate them. Hadley insists that having a right to regulate is "a very different thing" from knowing the right way to do it.

What actually sets the general level

"The necessity of competing in the markets of the world controls the general scale of railroad charges far more efficiently than any schedule can do." The real danger is not the average rate. It is "the specific abuses of power against a greater or lesser number of individual shippers."

Finding 6 · pp. 38–39 · Hadley's prescription

Equality and publicity

Leave the actual price to the railroad manager. Regulate how customers are treated and how the firm is managed.

Tool 1

Equality of treatment

At a minimum, prevent all personal discrimination: "equality of treatment should be secured to all persons under substantially like circumstances." How far to go against local differences or unequal classification "can only be decided by long experience."

Tool 2

Publicity of management

Even more important in practice. The worst abuses served "inside rings" and hurt stockholders as much as the public. In the long run, investors want flourishing traffic and shippers want better facilities, so their interests line up. An inside ring sacrifices both. "Nothing prevents this sacrifice so well as enforced publicity of management."

Fig. 8 · Personal-discrimination checkerHadley's minimum test

A strong road beats a weak one

A strong road or combination "is easier to hold responsible" and "far easier to make pursue a far-sighted policy." A bankrupt road "lives for the day only." Encouraging bankrupt roads to compete drags the strong ones down too: the policy of the whole group is set "by the practice of the worst individual."

The conclusion

"Where responsibility and equality of treatment can only be secured by combination, regulated combination is better than irresponsible competition."

France, Germany and England were years ahead of the U.S. here.

Step 4 · pp. 39–44 · the generalization

Factories are next

Any industry with large permanent investment has general expenses that "remain, to some extent, the same, whether the business increases or diminishes." That puts it under what Prof. H. C. Adams called the law of increasing returns: big producers win, price wars ignore fixed charges, and "such competition ends in combination." Hadley's examples are Standard Oil, the coal combinations, and the steel-rail business (H. D. Lloyd had already documented many more).

Hyndman's point against George: nationalizing land changes little "if capital organization remained as it now is," because control of capital "gives virtual control of the business, howsoever the land is held."

"Virtual monopoly" is enough

Upper Mississippi railroads had "no legal monopoly and little natural monopoly." Regulation was justified anyway, because they were monopolies in fact. The Court described public-use cases as "virtual monopoly," a test broad enough to reach many factories. "Should a serious exigency arise, it is not likely that the wide door here thrown open would remain unused."

The danger of business secrets

Most people believe manufacturers' profits form a huge fund that could raise wages if divided. It is "easy to show that this is not true in the aggregate," but workers won't believe it while their firm keeps its accounts secret. Railroads found that enforced publicity protected legitimate owners. A firm afraid of informing public opinion "may well be much more afraid of the risk of facing an outbreak of unenlightened public sentiment."

"The socialists, on the mere statement of the case, demand State ownership; but, to any one who is familiar with the abuses of State-owned monopolies, there is not even a prima facie ground for such an inference."Hadley, p. 43
